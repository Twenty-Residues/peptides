# Voice — Peptides.info

The writer behind Peptides.info came up writing conversion copy for a premium
peptide store (think peptidesciences.com): benefit-first, scannable, confident,
allergic to filler. That instinct stays — but here it's pointed at *understanding*
instead of *checkout*. We sell nothing. We're bullish on the science and honest
about the frontier.

## The register

- **Lead with the hook, back it with the tier.** Every entry opens with a
  punchy one-liner (the copywriter's job), then earns it with tiered, cited
  evidence (the reference's job). The hook can be bold; the claim can't overreach.
- **Benefit-forward, never hype-blind.** "Studied for X" beats "cures X." We use
  active verbs and concrete outcomes, but we never launder a Tier 3 animal study
  into a promise.
- **Peer voice, no hand-holding.** The reader is a smart, curious adult who can
  handle a mechanism and a caveat in the same sentence. No fear-mongering, no
  COA-anxiety, no "consult your doctor" boilerplate stapled to every line.
- **The frontier is exciting, not scary.** Where evidence is thin, we say so
  plainly and frame it as open territory worth watching — not "buyer beware."
- **Compliance is craft, not disclaimer soup.** This is a research and education
  reference. We describe what's been *studied*, never how to dose or use. Approval
  status is a fact we state, not a warning we hide behind.

## Mechanics

- Hooks: one line, ~10 words, a real claim or tension. No "unlock your potential."
- Summaries: 2–3 sentences, mechanism + what it's studied for + honest status.
- Dashes: spaced en dash ( – ) for asides; one em dash per page, max.
- Never: "miracle," "guaranteed," "cutting-edge" as filler, dosing, medical advice.

## The news desk

News is where neutrality is easiest to lose, so here it is enforced by the
shape of the story rather than the tone of the writer.

- **Three blocks, always.** What the record establishes. What it does not
  establish. What would change this. The second block is the one that earns
  trust from both camps.
- **Grade every source.** *Primary* is the document itself – a court filing,
  an agency letter, a trial registry, the company's own release. *Secondary* is
  a report about it. A story runs only when at least one primary record exists.
  Tallies, quotes, or figures that come only from reporting say so in the text.
- **Headlines state the fact, not the take.** "FDA sends warning letters to
  five sellers" – not "FDA cracks down." "28.3% mean weight loss, 11.3% quit
  the top dose" – both numbers, same line.
- **One open question per story.** Phrased so an enthusiast and a skeptic can
  each pick it up and argue. We do not answer it. We never hint at the answer.
- **Symmetry.** A claim of harm gets the same scrutiny as a claim of benefit.
  "Poisoned" in a prosecutor's statement is the prosecutor's word, and we say so.
- **Status and history.** Every story carries developing / updated / settled
  record, a published date, and a logged changelog when anything substantive
  moves.
- **Never.** Vendor placements, affiliate links, "alternatives" lists, AI
  illustrations presented as news imagery, anonymous bylines on contested facts,
  or the words safe, effective, scam, or useless in our own voice.
